  <h1>Stakeholder Power–Interest Matrix</h1>
  <p class="lead">Use this matrix to choose an engagement strategy. It helps separate “important title” from “important for this change.”</p>

  <section>
    <h2>What this method is for</h2>
    <p>Plot stakeholders by two dimensions: how much influence they have over the outcome and how much interest they have in the change. The result guides communication, involvement, and escalation.</p>
    <p>The matrix is not a political label. Power and interest are specific to the change and can move over time.</p>
  </section>

  <section>
    <h2>Four practical zones</h2>
    <div class="table-scroll">
      <table class="study-table">
        <thead><tr><th>Power</th><th>Interest</th><th>Default approach</th><th>Main risk</th></tr></thead>
        <tbody>
          <tr><td>High</td><td>High</td><td>Manage closely; involve in key decisions</td><td>Late disagreement can stop the change</td></tr>
          <tr><td>High</td><td>Low</td><td>Keep satisfied; use concise decision-level updates</td><td>They become active only when risk reaches them</td></tr>
          <tr><td>Low</td><td>High</td><td>Keep informed; use them for operational evidence</td><td>Their knowledge is ignored because authority is low</td></tr>
          <tr><td>Low</td><td>Low</td><td>Monitor with lightweight communication</td><td>Over-communication creates noise</td></tr>
        </tbody>
      </table>
    </div>
  </section>

  <section>
    <h2>Working method</h2>
    <ol>
      <li><strong>Define the change.</strong> Power is meaningless without a specific decision, process, or implementation.</li>
      <li><strong>Build the stakeholder list.</strong> Use process roles, system owners, users, governance, operations, external parties, and affected teams.</li>
      <li><strong>Rate influence with evidence.</strong> Ask what the stakeholder can approve, block, fund, configure, operate, or escalate.</li>
      <li><strong>Rate interest with evidence.</strong> Ask how much their work, risk, KPI, or customer outcome changes.</li>
      <li><strong>Place each stakeholder.</strong> Record uncertainty instead of pretending the rating is exact.</li>
      <li><strong>Choose an engagement action.</strong> Meeting, workshop, review, update, interview, demo, approval, or no active action.</li>
      <li><strong>Revisit at phase changes.</strong> A stakeholder may be low-interest during design and high-interest during cutover.</li>
    </ol>
  </section>

  <section>
    <h2>SAP example — new delivery-block automation</h2>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Sales Operations Lead — high power / high interest:</strong> owns commercial process and must validate the new rule.</li>
      <li><strong>Finance Director — high power / low interest:</strong> needs assurance that credit and revenue controls are not weakened.</li>
      <li><strong>Customer Service Users — low power / high interest:</strong> provide critical evidence about exceptions and daily work.</li>
      <li><strong>Unrelated regional team — low power / low interest:</strong> monitor unless rollout scope changes.</li>
    </ul>
    <p>The key Lead behavior is to protect the operational voice even when it has less formal power.</p>
  </section>

  <section>
    <h2>Decision rules</h2>
    <ul>
      <li>If a stakeholder can block the change, treat that as high influence even if they are not senior.</li>
      <li>If a stakeholder owns operational evidence, involve them in discovery even when formal power is low.</li>
      <li>If interest is based only on curiosity, do not confuse it with impact.</li>
      <li>If the matrix changes after a scope or rollout decision, update the engagement plan.</li>
      <li>If the next problem is ownership of activities, continue with <a href="/skill-hub/methods-catalog/raci-matrix/">RACI</a>.</li>
    </ul>
  </section>

  <section>
    <h2>Copy-ready template</h2>
    <pre><code>| Stakeholder / Role | Evidence of Power | Evidence of Interest | Zone | Engagement | Cadence | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| &lt;role&gt; | &lt;approve/block/fund/operate&gt; | &lt;impact/KPI/risk&gt; | High/High | workshop + decision review | weekly | &lt;role&gt; |
| &lt;role&gt; | ... | ... | Low/High | interviews + demos | milestone | &lt;role&gt; |</code></pre>
  </section>

  <section>
    <h2>Quality checklist</h2>
    <ul>
      <li>Ratings are tied to a specific change.</li>
      <li>Power and interest have evidence, not intuition only.</li>
      <li>Operational users are not excluded because they lack formal authority.</li>
      <li>Every high-power stakeholder has an engagement action.</li>
      <li>The matrix is reviewed when scope or project phase changes.</li>
    </ul>
  </section>

  <section>
    <h2>Common mistakes</h2>
    <ul>
      <li><strong>Equating job grade with power.</strong> A specialist may control a required approval or critical data.</li>
      <li><strong>Treating low-power stakeholders as low-value.</strong> They often know the real process and exceptions.</li>
      <li><strong>Publishing sensitive labels carelessly.</strong> Keep the artifact professional and evidence-based.</li>
      <li><strong>Using the matrix once at kickoff.</strong> Stakeholder dynamics change during design, testing, and go-live.</li>
    </ul>
  </section>

  <section>
    <h2>Agent instructions</h2>
    <p>An AI agent should not infer influence from seniority or interest from meeting attendance. It should list the evidence used for each placement and flag uncertain ratings for human review.</p>

  <section>
    <h2>Status and limitations</h2>
    <p>This is a simple engagement model. It does not explain stakeholder motivation, trust, conflict, or organizational politics by itself. Use it as a routing aid, then apply deeper stakeholder analysis where needed.</p>
