Enterprise Context / SAP TM

Transportation demand is not a freight plan.

SAP Transportation Management sits between the business need to move goods and the transport that is actually planned, executed, and settled. The useful chain is demand → Freight Unit → freight document → execution → charges → settlement. Each step creates a different business state, so a green status in one step does not prove that the next one exists.

Demand is not the plan. A sales order or purchase order explains why goods should move. An OTR or DTR represents transportation demand. A Freight Unit is a planning unit. A Freight Order or Freight Booking represents planned transportation capacity and execution. A Freight Settlement Document carries freight-cost settlement into Materials Management.

Most TM investigations become easier once we identify which of those states is the first one that is missing or wrong.

Transportation demand

TM starts by translating an ERP document into transport demand.

Order and delivery integration let TM plan against business documents from Sales, Materials Management, and Logistics Execution. The first important distinction is whether planning starts from the order or from the delivery.

Order-Based Transportation Requirement

An OTR represents transportation demand from an order. It allows transportation planning to begin before the final delivery exists. In SAP's document model, the OTR corresponds to the source order and can produce Freight Units for planning.

Delivery-Based Transportation Requirement

A DTR represents demand from a delivery. Where order-based planning is also used, delivery-based demand can consume the Freight Units that came from the related OTR. This is a transition of demand, not a second independent shipment that should be planned again.

The design choice changes when transportation becomes visible and which document changes can still affect the plan. It is therefore better to ask “which business document currently owns the transportation demand?” than to assume that every process always passes through both requirement types in the same way.

Planning unit

The Freight Unit is the handoff from demand to planning.

TM does not normally plan a sales-order item or delivery item directly. Freight Unit Building turns transportation-relevant quantities into units that the planner can assign to transport capacity.

The Freight Unit Building rule controls how demand is grouped or split. SAP supports strategies that can consolidate demand strongly, keep units closer to individual items, or group compatible parts of one request. That choice affects planning freedom: too much fragmentation creates unnecessary planning objects, while overly large units can remove useful consolidation options.

A Freight Unit also carries transportation dates and locations that planning must respect. If the planned route or timing looks unreasonable, check the demand and Freight Unit before changing optimizer settings. The planner can only work with the world represented by those objects.

Transportation planning

Planning assigns transportable demand to executable capacity.

Manual planning and optimization work with Freight Units, locations, dates, resources, capacities, network data, costs, and constraints. Their result is not another demand object; it is a freight document that describes how transportation should be executed.

TM uses Freight Orders and Freight Bookings as freight documents. A Freight Booking specifically represents reserved freight space on a ship or in an airplane and is also used during execution. Freight Orders cover other freight-order scenarios and are the central object in many road processes. In multimodal designs, several freight documents can therefore participate in one end-to-end movement.

This is why “the Freight Unit exists” and “the transport is planned” are different statements. A Freight Unit can still be unplanned, partly planned, or assigned differently across its stages. The planning result must be read at the relevant stage and freight-document level.

Carrier commitment

A feasible plan does not mean a carrier has accepted the work.

Carrier selection and tendering sit after the planning question. They decide who should perform the transport and whether that carrier has actually committed to it.

Carrier selection can use configured eligibility and ranking information. Tendering then supports a commercial conversation around a freight request for quotation, carrier responses, and award or rejection. SAP supports several tendering strategies, including direct and broadcast patterns. The important boundary is simple: planning creates a transport proposal; tendering resolves external commitment.

That distinction matters operationally. A freight document may contain a route and dates while the subcontracting or tendering state is still unresolved. Treating “planned” as “accepted by the carrier” hides a real business dependency.

Warehouse execution

TM owns transportation; EWM owns detailed warehouse work.

The two products meet around deliveries, loading, unloading, quantities, and transport execution, but they do not become one execution engine.

SAP supports more than one TM–EWM integration pattern. In Advanced Shipping and Receiving with embedded components, the process is based on the TM Freight Order and does not use the EWM transportation unit or vehicle. SAP also documents direct integration based on deliveries and EWM transportation units, where TM planning results are sent to EWM and warehouse execution results such as loading or quantity discrepancies return to TM.

That difference is important when diagnosing status mismatches. First identify the integration pattern, then ask whether the disputed state belongs to transport execution or warehouse execution. Applying an Advanced Shipping and Receiving assumption to an EWM-TU scenario can send the investigation to the wrong object.

Charges and settlement

Transport execution and freight-cost settlement finish on different tracks.

TM can calculate transportation charges on freight documents, but a calculated amount is not yet a supplier invoice and not yet a completed financial process.

For freight settlement, TM creates a Freight Settlement Document and posts it to Materials Management. SAP uses the settlement information to create the purchasing follow-on documents, including a purchase order and service entry sheet. The confirmed service entry then provides the basis for invoice verification or evaluated receipt settlement in MM.

Execution status can also participate in freight-cost confirmation before settlement is posted. This keeps an important boundary visible: a truck can finish its physical journey while cost confirmation, the Freight Settlement Document, or the MM follow-on process is still incomplete.

A practical trace

“The shipment happened, but the freight cost is not ready for invoice verification.”

This symptom crosses several TM states, so starting from the invoice is usually too late in the chain.

First prove that the correct transportation demand became the intended Freight Units and freight document. Then separate carrier commitment from physical execution: the transport may have been planned without the expected tendering result, or executed while a required event or cost-confirmation state is still missing. If execution is complete, move to charge calculation and the Freight Settlement Document. Only after the FSD is posted does the investigation move into the MM purchasing and invoice-verification follow-on.

The value of this sequence is not the order of screens. It is the change of business ownership: source document → TM demand → planning → carrier and execution → TM settlement → MM follow-on. The first incorrect state tells us which layer deserves attention.
