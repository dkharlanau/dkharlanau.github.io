Enterprise Context Lab / Shipping & Scheduling

A delivery date is the result of several logistics decisions.

SAP does not calculate a customer delivery date from one lead-time field. The sales document supplies a delivering plant and business context; Shipping determines an execution point and route; scheduling works backward or forward through several activities; availability confirms what can actually be promised; and delivery creation starts the physical execution process.

Follow the logistics chain arrow_downward
local_shipping

Keep the layers separate: plant, shipping point, route, scheduling dates, ATP confirmation, outbound delivery, warehouse work, and transportation planning describe different decisions.

A late confirmed date can originate in Shipping, but it can also be the correct result of an availability constraint. A correct sales-order date does not prove that the order is ready for delivery creation or that warehouse and transport execution can finish on time.

The core model

From a sales requirement to an executable delivery.

The useful model is a chain of ownership. Each step contributes information that the next step can use, but no single step owns the whole promise.

Sales requirement→ Delivering plant→ Shipping point + route→ Scheduling + ATP→ Delivery due→ Outbound delivery

The sales order already contains important shipping inputs, but delivery creation is not a passive copy. SAP can re-evaluate delivery quantities and availability, redetermine the route, check scheduling, determine the picking location, and update order status while the outbound delivery is created.

Shipping point

The shipping point defines where the delivery is processed.

A shipping point is an organizational unit for shipping execution. SAP can determine it automatically for a sales-order item. The exact determination model is configurable: a plant can be sufficient, while a more differentiated setup can also use the ship-to party's shipping condition and the material's loading group.

Delivering plant

The plant is an upstream decision. If it is wrong, Shipping starts from the wrong physical context before route or scheduling logic is even evaluated.

Shipping condition

This represents the agreed shipping treatment. It can come from the ship-to party, and a sales document type can also supply a shipping condition that takes precedence in the documented standard behavior.

Loading group

The material master can classify how the material is normally loaded, for example manually or by forklift. In a multi-shipping-point design, this can participate in shipping-point determination.

One outbound delivery is processed through one shipping point. If order items resolve to different shipping points, they cannot simply be forced into the same standard delivery. SAP also documents that the shipping point is no longer editable after the delivery has been created, so an alternative shipping point must be selected earlier in the order or stock transport order process.

Route

The route connects geography, transport characteristics, and scheduling.

In classic Sales route determination, SAP uses the departure context of the shipping point, the destination context of the ship-to party, the shipping condition, and the material's transportation group. The result is more than a descriptive route: route data influences transportation scheduling.

Departure zone+ Destination zone+ Shipping condition+ Transportation group→ Route

The route proposed in the sales order is not necessarily final. Depending on delivery configuration, SAP can determine the route again when the delivery is created. At that point the total delivery weight is known, so the weight group can participate as an additional factor. In the sales order a route can differ by item; in the delivery one route applies to all items.

Delivery and transportation scheduling

Four durations create several operational dates.

It is useful to stop saying “lead time” and name the activity. Standard delivery scheduling distinguishes transit time, loading time, pick/pack time, and transportation lead time. Each describes a different part of getting the goods from availability to the customer.

Transit time

How long the goods need to travel from the shipping point to the customer. SAP determines this from the route.

Loading time

How long loading takes. The documented determination can use shipping point, route, and the material's loading group.

Pick/pack time

How long the goods need to be picked and packed before loading. Shipping-point and product/order characteristics can influence this duration.

Transportation lead time

How early transportation must be organized, for example to reserve a truck or other capacity. SAP determines this from the route.

Backward scheduling starts from the requested delivery date. It subtracts transit time to reach the goods issue date, loading time to reach the loading date, and pick/pack time to reach the material availability date. Transportation scheduling also establishes when transport planning must start. If backward scheduling reaches an infeasible point, forward scheduling determines the earliest feasible dates instead.

Calendars and working times matter independently of the stored durations. A weekend, a closed shipping window, or different carrier working days can move the result even when no lead-time value changed.

ATP boundary

Scheduling constructs the requirement date; ATP tests the promise.

Shipping scheduling and availability checking are tightly connected but they answer different questions. Scheduling calculates when the material must be available for the requested logistics path. ATP or aATP then evaluates whether the requested quantity can be confirmed for the relevant date under the configured availability and scarcity rules.

A date problem can therefore masquerade as a stock problem. If the material availability date entering ATP is already too early or too late because plant, route, duration, or calendar logic is wrong, the availability check can be technically correct and still produce an unwanted business result.

Delivery due and creation

A confirmed order still has to become due and pass delivery checks.

SAP documents a schedule line as due for shipping when the relevant material-availability or transportation-scheduling date is reached. The order, item, and schedule line must also satisfy delivery prerequisites. A header delivery block, for example, can prevent creation even when the dates look correct.

When the outbound delivery is created, SAP starts the shipping process rather than merely freezing the sales order into another document. Standard processing can check the order and material, determine the delivery quantity, check availability, calculate weight and volume, redetermine the route, check delivery scheduling, determine a picking location, perform batch determination where required, and update sales-order status.

Items are combined only when the shipping criteria are compatible. A split therefore has a business or execution reason somewhere in the document context; it should not be treated as a random document-generation problem.

Warehouse and transportation boundaries

The outbound delivery is the handoff, not the end of execution.

After delivery creation, detailed warehouse and transportation processes can have their own objects, statuses, dates, and decisions. This is where a simple SD-only mental model becomes misleading.

EWMWarehouse executionEWM owns detailed warehouse requests, warehouse tasks, warehouse orders, stock positioning, picking, staging, packing, and related execution decisions for EWM-managed processes.arrow_forward TMTransportation planningTM can consume order- or delivery-based transportation demand, build planning units, create executable freight plans, and coordinate transport execution.arrow_forward

SAP's current S/4HANA documentation also describes tighter TM/EWM integration through Advanced Shipping and Receiving. In such scenarios, warehouse requests and handling units in EWM and freight units in TM participate in one integrated execution flow. That does not make SD, EWM, and TM the same layer; it makes ownership boundaries more important.

A compact diagnostic example

Why “the customer date is wrong” is not yet a root cause.

Assume an order is confirmed two days later than expected. The stock figure looks healthy, so the first reaction is to blame ATP. But the material availability date is already one day later because the order received an unexpected route, and another day is added because the relevant shipping calendar is closed. ATP is only confirming against the date it received.

The useful fix is not to relax availability or overwrite the final date. It is to identify why the shipping input changed: wrong plant, shipping point, route, duration, or calendar. Once that first divergence is corrected, scheduling and the availability check can be evaluated again on the intended business path.
