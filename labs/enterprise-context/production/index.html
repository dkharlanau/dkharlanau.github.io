Manufacturing / Production Planning & Execution

MRP proposes supply.
Production turns it into executable work.

Plan-to-Produce is not one production-order transaction. Demand creates requirements, MRP proposes how to cover them, production master data defines a feasible method, and execution records what actually happened. The useful skill is seeing where one decision becomes the next object.

Follow the production flow arrow_downward
precision_manufacturing

Scope: this page uses discrete production orders as the main example. Process manufacturing, repetitive manufacturing, Kanban and PP/DS share parts of the planning model but have different execution objects and control details. Do not transfer one scenario's transactions or status behavior to another without checking the product documentation.

From demand to executable supply

The production order starts after several planning decisions have already been made.

MRP begins with requirements and the supply that can cover them. The planning run performs net-requirements calculation against stock and relevant receipts. If the requirement is not covered, it creates a procurement proposal. For a material planned for in-house production, that proposal is commonly a planned order.

A planned order is still a planning object. It can be changed by planning logic, firmed, split or converted according to the scenario. When the decision becomes executable, an in-house planned order can be converted to a production order. A production order can also be created directly, so the existence of an order does not prove that it originated from MRP.

Requirement

Sales demand, planned independent requirements, dependent requirements and other planning elements define what needs supply and when.

Planning proposal

MRP evaluates stock, receipts, procurement settings, lot sizing and dates, then proposes supply where a shortage remains.

Execution order

The production order carries the material, quantity, dates, operations, components, statuses and cost-collection context needed for shop-floor execution.

Production method

A material number is not enough to explain how the product will be made.

The bill of material defines the component structure. The routing defines the operations and their sequence for discrete manufacturing, while work centers provide resource, scheduling, capacity and costing context. A production version binds a valid BOM alternative to a task list or recipe for a material and plant. It also carries validity and lot-size ranges, so the same material can have more than one legitimate production method.

This distinction becomes important when an order contains the wrong components or operations. Editing the order may fix one execution instance, but it does not explain why that structure was selected. First check the production version and its validity, the selected BOM and routing, and whether the master data changed between planning and order creation. SAP also provides consistency checks for production versions because a version can exist while its assigned BOM or task list is no longer consistent for the full validity or lot-size range.

Execution

Release, material availability and confirmation describe different parts of production.

Once the order is ready, scheduling determines operation dates and capacity requirements. Release makes the order available for the configured execution steps. Components then need to be available where production can use them. A reservation or plant-level stock quantity is not the same as physical availability at the production supply area.

Goods issue records component withdrawal and assigns the material cost to the manufacturing order. Depending on the design, the issue can be posted explicitly or through backflush during confirmation. Confirmation records production progress such as yield, scrap, work and the work center used. It may also trigger configured goods movements, but confirmation itself should not be treated as proof that every related goods movement succeeded.

That separation matters in support. An order can be confirmed while a backflush or automatic goods receipt has failed or is still pending. The right reconciliation is between the confirmation, material documents, order quantities and the resulting stock state, not merely the order status.

Warehouse boundary

Production demand and warehouse execution meet at the supply point.

If the components are managed in EWM, the warehouse can own the physical staging to a production supply area. SAP supports several integration patterns, including delivery-based production supply and embedded scenarios with tighter synchronous handling. The correct question is therefore not simply whether stock exists, but whether the required quantity has reached the production supply context through the intended warehouse process.

The same caution applies to the finished product. Receipt from production can involve Inventory Management only or an EWM inbound process, depending on deployment and configuration. A shop-floor completion signal is not enough evidence that the finished quantity is available in the expected warehouse or stock type.

For the inventory side of that handoff, continue with Inventory Management. For physical warehouse execution, use the EWM model.

Planning variants

Make-to-stock and make-to-order change demand and ownership, not the meaning of every PP object.

In make-to-stock, production usually replenishes general stock against forecast and demand consumption logic. In make-to-order, the customer requirement can drive order-specific planning, stock segmentation and cost behavior. The exact result depends on the planning strategy and account-assignment design, so “MTO” alone does not tell us how every stock or cost object will behave.

PP/DS adds another planning boundary. When a planned order is managed in PP/DS, SAP recommends conversion from PP/DS so detailed planning dates are preserved in the manufacturing order. Converting that same order in S/4HANA can lose PP/DS detailed scheduling information. This is a scenario-specific rule, not a reason to treat every S/4HANA planned-order conversion as a PP/DS process.

Where problems cross boundaries

A production symptom often belongs to another object before it belongs to the order.

Short component

Separate the requirement from supply. MRP may have planned correctly while purchasing, another production order or warehouse staging is late. Conversely, material may exist in the plant but not at the production supply area.

Wrong structure

Trace production version, validity, lot size, BOM and routing before changing components or operations manually in the order.

Late order

Separate requirement date, basic scheduling, operation durations, work-center capacity and any PP/DS decision. A material-planning parameter cannot solve a real resource bottleneck.

A useful investigation therefore starts from the first wrong state. If MRP created the wrong quantity, repair the demand or planning cause. If the order copied the wrong method, repair the relevant master data or selection rule. If execution diverged from the plan, preserve the production event and correct the execution or posting problem rather than rewriting history with unexplained compensating movements.

Cost and close

Physical completion and financial completion are related, but they are not the same state.

During execution, the manufacturing order can collect component, activity and other actual costs. Goods receipt credits the order according to the valuation design. What remains is handled through the applicable product-costing model: work in process, variance calculation and settlement can be period-based or order-based, and SAP S/4HANA Cloud also has event-based production cost posting scenarios.

The settlement rule defines which portions of a sender's costs go to which receivers. In traditional Product Cost by Order or Product Cost by Period scenarios, the settlement type and period-end design determine how WIP and variances are handled. This is why a technically completed order can still require financial processing, and why a remaining balance should be explained rather than cleared by assumption.

A compact way to read a production issue

Follow the object chain and prove the first divergence.

Suppose a production order for 100 pieces contains the wrong component. The order itself tells us what will execute, but not why that structure was chosen. Start with the requirement and planned order, identify the production version used for the relevant date and lot size, compare its BOM and routing with the order, and check whether master data changed before conversion. Only then decide whether the order is an intentional exception or evidence of a master-data problem.

The same method works downstream. If 100 pieces were confirmed but only 95 appear in stock, compare confirmation quantities, goods-movement records, EWM receipt where relevant, and reversals. If logistics is complete but the order still carries cost, move to WIP, variance and settlement evidence. Each layer has its own proof.
