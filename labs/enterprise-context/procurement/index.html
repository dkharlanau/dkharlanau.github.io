Deep vertical / Procurement

Procurement starts with a requirement.
The PO records one controlled commitment.

In SAP S/4HANA, procurement connects demand, source-of-supply logic, commercial terms, purchasing controls, receipt or acceptance, supplier invoicing, and financial reconciliation. A purchase order matters because it brings those decisions together, not because it is the whole process.

Follow the process arrow_downward
shopping_cart

Baseline: requirement → source of supply → purchase order → receipt or acceptance → supplier invoice → reconciliation.

That sequence is a useful reference, not a universal document recipe. Stock transfer, consignment, subcontracting, services, third-party procurement, and other variants deliberately change which events are required.

From demand to commitment

Read procurement as a chain of decisions that become documents.

A requirement says what the business needs, where it is needed, in what quantity, and by when. The requirement may come from planning, a manual purchase requisition, a project, maintenance, production, or another process. A purchase requisition can formalize that internal demand before Purchasing turns it into an external commitment.

The purchase order then combines organizational responsibility, supplier, items, quantities, dates, commercial conditions, item behavior, and accounting context. Some values are entered, some are copied from a preceding document, and some are determined from master data or configuration. That is why a wrong value in the PO is often the visible result of an earlier decision rather than the place where the defect started.

Requirement

Prove the material or service, plant or receiving context, quantity, required date, and consumption intent before changing purchasing data.

Commitment

Establish which source is valid, which terms apply, how the item should behave, and whether approval is required before the order is sent.

Execution

Follow receipt or service acceptance, invoice verification, and the financial state that remains after those events.

SAP's current Create Purchase Requisition - Advanced documentation describes the requisition as the object used to request materials, services, and limits with the required quantity, location, and timing. The exact user interface can differ by deployment and process, but the business distinction between internal requirement and purchasing commitment remains useful.

Source of supply

Supplier selection is a constrained choice, not a simple vendor lookup.

Source determination has to be read in the exact purchasing context. Material, plant, requirement date, purchasing organization, and maintained source relationships can all matter. A source list can make a source fixed or block it for a validity period. A quota arrangement can distribute requirements across several sources. Contracts, scheduling agreements, and purchasing info records provide additional source and commercial context.

This is why a fixed sequence such as “quota first, then source list, then info record” is a poor mental model. These objects have different jobs and interact through the configured source-determination process. Start with the result SAP produced and reconstruct which valid source objects existed for that material, plant, date, and purchasing organization.

For example, if Supplier A looks correct in the info record but the requisition is assigned to Supplier B, the difference may be intentional: a valid source-list record can mark B as fixed, or a quota arrangement can allocate part of the requirement to B. Manually replacing the supplier in the PO may hide the real sourcing rule.

Item and accounting context

Item category and account assignment answer different questions.

The purchasing item category controls important process behavior. SAP documents, among other things, whether the item needs a material number, whether account assignment is required, whether it is managed as stock, and whether goods receipt and invoice receipt are expected. That is why the item category changes the later document flow rather than merely changing how the item is displayed.

Account assignment answers a different question: where should the value be charged when the purchase is for consumption rather than ordinary valuated stock? Depending on the process, the receiver can be a cost center, order, project, asset, sales-order context, or another supported object. SAP also supports multiple account assignment for one purchasing item where the cost has to be distributed.

Keep the two layers separate. A wrong cost center does not prove the source-of-supply logic is wrong. A correct account assignment does not prove the item has the intended receipt and invoice behavior.

Commercial terms

The PO price belongs to a purchasing context.

A purchase-order price can be influenced by the selected supplier, reference document, purchasing conditions, order unit, quantity scales, and validity date. Purchasing info records can hold conditions by order unit, and SAP can use those conditions during source determination and PO creation. Contracts and other purchasing documents can carry their own condition logic as well.

This matters when a supplier change and a price change appear together. The two results may have the same upstream cause, but they are still different decisions. First prove why the source was chosen. Then prove which condition record, reference, order unit, scale, and validity period produced the commercial value. A manual net-price overwrite can make the current PO look right while leaving the next PO wrong for exactly the same reason.

SAP's current Conditions in Info Records documentation explicitly distinguishes the net price in the info record from the net price in the purchase order and describes conditions maintained for different order units.

Purchase-order control

A complete PO still has to become an executable commitment.

The purchase order brings the sourcing and commercial decisions into a supplier-facing document, but it can still be subject to approval and output controls. In current SAP S/4HANA, flexible workflow is available for purchase requisitions and purchase orders alongside other approval mechanisms that may exist in a customer landscape. The active model therefore has to be identified before an approval problem is diagnosed.

Keep document validity, approval, and supplier communication distinct. A PO can be structurally correct but still wait for approval. An approved PO can still have a communication problem. Conversely, receiving a supplier response does not prove that the accounting or receipt design of the item is correct.

Receipt and acceptance

Execution depends on what the item was designed to represent.

For a standard stock purchase, goods receipt records that the ordered material arrived and updates the relevant inventory and purchase-order history. For direct consumption, the same business event can post value to an assigned object instead of ordinary stock. Service procurement can introduce an acceptance or service-entry step. Special item categories intentionally change the baseline again.

SAP's standard item-category model makes this visible. Standard items can have both goods and invoice receipts. Stock-transfer items expect a goods receipt but no supplier invoice receipt. Consignment items receive supplier-owned stock without a normal PO invoice receipt at that point. Subcontracting adds the provided-component relationship to the receipt process. The useful question is therefore not “Why is there no GR or IR?” until the intended item category and process variant are known.

If warehouse execution or quality inspection sits between physical arrival and unrestricted stock, continue with the Inventory Management, EWM, or Quality Management guide rather than forcing those details into the PO model.

Supplier invoice

Invoice verification compares a commercial claim with the purchasing history.

The supplier invoice is a separate business event from receipt. With purchase-order reference, SAP can allocate an invoice to purchasing documents and, depending on the scenario, to related references such as delivery notes or service entry sheets. Quantity, value, tax, and tolerance checks determine whether the invoice can be posted cleanly or needs exception handling.

The reference model matters. Where goods-receipt-based invoice verification is active, the invoice is matched against the relevant goods receipts rather than only against the ordered quantity. Other procurement variants have different matching rules. Do not infer the intended invoice behavior from the existence of a PO alone.

See SAP's current Entering Invoices with Purchase Order Reference documentation for the supported allocation choices in Logistics Invoice Verification.

Financial reconciliation

GR/IR is a timing bridge, not a generic error bucket.

In procurement scenarios where goods receipt and invoice receipt are both value-relevant, the two events can occur on different dates or for different quantities. GR/IR carries that temporary difference. An open balance can therefore mean that the complementary event has not happened yet, not that the account is broken.

SAP's current GR/IR documentation makes the expectation explicit: if invoiced quantity is greater than received quantity, the system expects further goods receipts; if received quantity is greater than invoiced quantity, it expects further invoices. Manual clearing belongs to the case where the business has established that no further receipt or invoice will arrive.

This is where procurement hands the story to Finance. Continue with FI/CO for Logistics for valuation, G/L, GR/IR, and reconciliation, and with the supplier payment cluster once the question has moved from invoice liability to payment execution.

One connected example

The supplier changed, then the price changed too.

A buyer expects Supplier A for a material, but a requisition is converted with Supplier B and a higher PO price. It is tempting to repair both values directly in the PO. That produces a clean-looking document and weak evidence.

Start with the requirement context: material, plant, purchasing organization, quantity, and date. If the source list makes Supplier B fixed for that validity period, the supplier result is explained. Now inspect the commercial context for B. If the purchasing info record has conditions for a different order unit or a current scale that produces the higher value, the price result is explained too.

The important point is not the exact master-data combination. It is the separation of decisions. Source determination answers why B was selected. Price determination answers why B's commercial value was used. If the business expected A, fix the source rule or its validity. If B is correct but the price is wrong, fix the relevant purchasing condition or reference. Editing both outputs without proving either decision leaves the next requirement exposed to the same defect.

Process variants

Use standard external procurement as the baseline, then name what changes.

The standard material flow is useful because it shows all major boundaries: requirement, external supplier, PO, goods receipt, invoice receipt, and reconciliation. Special procurement is easier to understand when we compare each variant with that baseline instead of memorizing a separate list of transactions.

Stock transfer

The supplying source is internal. SAP's standard item category expects goods receipt but not a supplier invoice receipt.

Consignment

Material is received into separately managed supplier-owned stock. The ordinary PO receipt is not the invoice event.

Subcontracting

The supplier performs work using components provided by the customer. Receipt of the subcontracted material is connected to consumption of those provided components.

These are examples, not a complete catalogue. The item category, account assignment, stock ownership, acceptance model, and invoice design define which downstream events make sense for the actual process.
