Automotive / Just-In-Time and Just-In-Sequence

From forecast to the exact part, time, and sequence.

Automotive JIT works because planning and execution stay connected without becoming the same thing. Forecast and JIT delivery schedules tell a supplier what demand is expected. A customer JIT call turns that demand into a short-horizon request. A JIS call adds one more constraint: the components must arrive in the production sequence the customer requested.

The useful distinction: the scheduling agreement carries the longer-term commercial and planning relationship; delivery schedules provide forecast or near-term planning releases; JIT calls request concrete short-term supply. SAP's current Next Generation JIT documentation also distinguishes summarized, delivery-order, order-based, and sequenced JIT calls. JIS is the sequenced case, where delivery order matters as much as quantity and time.

Demand model

Start with the agreement, then narrow the time horizon.

The easiest way to understand automotive JIT is to follow how demand becomes more precise.

Scheduling agreement

The long-term relationship defines the customer, material, quantities, and release process. It is the commercial and planning base that the short-term flow depends on.

Forecast and JIT delivery schedules

These releases describe expected quantities and dates over a horizon. They help production and procurement plan before the exact call arrives.

Customer JIT or JIS call

The call requests supply in the short term. A sequenced JIT call adds the customer's production sequence, so the supplier must protect that order through preparation and delivery.

In classic JIT Inbound, SAP can compare received call requirements with forecast and JIT delivery-schedule requirements. A mismatch is therefore a planning-versus-execution problem, not automatically an interface failure.

Process families

Classic JIT and Next Generation JIT share the business idea, not one runtime model.

Both support short-horizon automotive supply, but their objects, applications, control logic, and integration options differ. Do not carry a transaction, app, or status assumption from one family into the other without checking the release and process.

Classic JIT Inbound

Classic processing centers on JIT calls, component groups, call components, internal and external status, actions, requirements matching, and JIT monitoring. It explicitly supports summarized and sequenced calls.

Classic JIT Outbound

A system supplier can forward relevant customer JIT requirements to upstream suppliers. This is a separate process boundary: the customer call and the supplier requirement must remain traceable without being treated as the same document.

Next Generation JIT Supply to Customer

The current S/4HANA process adds dedicated JIT master data and Fiori applications, broader call patterns, action control, sequence and packing concepts, and tighter logistics integration for supply to the customer.

Runtime

A call is only useful when the whole execution chain stays consistent.

For a real incident, we follow the call from business identity to physical completion instead of starting with a transaction code.

1. Identify the call in the correct business context

First establish which customer, destination, material or component group, scheduling agreement, external call number, requirement date and time, and call type belong together. A technically valid message mapped to the wrong destination is still the wrong business demand.

2. Let process control decide what can happen next

Classic JIT call control and Next Generation action control both connect business processing to status transitions and follow-on actions. The implementation details differ, but the principle is stable: status is not decoration. It determines which next step is valid and records what has already happened.

3. Compare execution demand with the planning baseline

In classic JIT Inbound, Requirements Match compares cumulative call requirements with relevant forecast and JIT delivery schedules. SAP classifies the result as critical, balanced, or not balanced. That makes the difference between a real short-term demand increase and a simple message-processing problem visible.

4. Protect sequence and packing where JIS requires it

A JIS call is tied to the customer's production order. The supplier therefore has to preserve sequence through production or picking, grouping, packing, and delivery. The sequence becomes operational data, not just an informational field.

5. Prove completion in logistics, not only in the JIT monitor

In the current Next Generation supply-to-customer flow, sequenced JIT calls can be used to create outbound deliveries. Delivery creation updates the call, and changes to status, quantity, or handling units in SAP EWM can be reflected back in the JIT call details. The final proof is therefore a consistent chain between the call, physical package, delivery, goods movement, and the required customer confirmation or downstream state.

Sequence

A missing sequence number is not automatically a missing part.

This is one of the easiest JIS mistakes to make when reading the data.

SAP's current sequence-number checks explicitly allow different rules: check without gaps, check with gaps, or no sequence check. Gaps can be legitimate when an OEM splits consecutive production-sequence numbers across several suppliers. One supplier might receive 1, 2, 5, and 7 while another receives 3, 4, and 6.

That means a sequence problem should be judged against the configured delivery-sequence rule and the actual supplier scope. Treating every gap as lost demand can lead to unnecessary manual correction and can make the sequence worse.

Diagnosis

Find the first broken handoff.

Most useful diagnoses separate the demand signal, call state, sequence, and physical execution instead of grouping everything under “JIT failed.”

Call exceeds the schedule baseline

Check the latest forecast and JIT delivery schedules, cumulative quantities, call timing, and customer changes before blaming the interface. The call may be valid and the planning picture may simply be behind.

Sequence looks broken

Check which delivery-sequence rule applies and whether gaps are expected for this supplier before changing sequence data. Then inspect packing and delayed/subsequent-delivery handling.

Delivery exists but the business flow is not complete

Compare call status, delivery and handling-unit state, goods movement, and confirmation evidence. A created delivery proves only one step of the JIT/JIS lifecycle.

Architecture boundary

JIT coordinates demand; the surrounding logistics applications execute it.

Keep ownership clear when the process crosses application boundaries.

The JIT/JIS layer owns the short-term call, its processing state, and—where relevant—the sequence context. Delivery Management owns the delivery document and shipping execution. Handling units describe the physical package. EWM owns warehouse execution when an EWM-managed flow is used. TM owns transportation planning and execution. The integration must carry enough JIT context across those boundaries to keep the call and the physical result traceable.

This is also why “the message arrived” is not a useful completion criterion. Automotive JIT succeeds only when the intended short-term requirement becomes the intended physical supply in the right quantity, at the right time and, for JIS, in the right sequence.
