Deep vertical / Quality Management

Quality is a decision chain.
The inspection lot holds it together.

SAP Quality Management connects a business event to an inspection, the inspection to recorded evidence, and that evidence to a usage decision. Stock can be part of the process, but it is not the process itself. The useful skill is knowing which quality object explains the business state you see.

Follow the inspection flow arrow_downward
fact_check

Start with why the inspection exists. An inspection lot can be created automatically from a logistics or production event, or manually for a separate quality task. The inspection lot origin describes that context; the inspection type and inspection setup control how the material is inspected.

Do not equate the usage decision with a stock movement. The usage decision records the quality disposition. A stock posting is a separate consequence and is available only for stock-relevant inspection lots.

Core flow

Five ideas explain most inspection processes.

The screens differ across procurement, production, sales, and warehouse scenarios. The underlying chain is more stable.

01

Trigger

A goods receipt, production-order release, delivery, source inspection, or manual quality task creates the need to inspect.

The business event explains why the inspection starts.
02

Inspection lot

The lot is the control object for the inspection. Its origin and type place the inspection in a particular business context.

The lot connects the event to quality processing.
03

Inspection specification

Characteristics, procedures, sampling, and related master data define what must be checked and how the result is evaluated.

The specification defines the work, not the lot number alone.
04

Results and defects

Recorded results provide the evidence for valuation. Defects describe observed nonconformance when that level of detail is needed.

Evidence must exist before a reliable disposition can be made.
05

Usage decision and consequence

The usage decision accepts, rejects, or otherwise classifies the inspected quantity. Depending on the scenario, follow-up can affect stock, production, procurement, delivery, or customer quality evidence.

The decision and its logistics consequence are related, but they are not the same object.

Trigger and setup

The same material can be inspected for different reasons.

That is why starting from a transaction code is often misleading. First identify the event and the inspection context.

A purchased material may be inspected when it is received from a supplier. A manufactured material may be inspected during production and again when the finished quantity is received. A sales delivery can have its own inspection before goods leave the company. SAP also supports manually created lots for quality work that is not driven by a stock movement.

For automatic lot creation, the relevant inspection type must be active in the material's inspection setup and the business event must be connected to the appropriate inspection-lot origin. This distinction matters operationally: if a goods receipt was posted but no lot exists, the first question is whether the trigger and inspection setup matched. Results recording is not yet the problem.

Inspection specification

The lot is the container. The specification defines what has to be proven.

A lot without the right inspection specification can exist technically and still fail to support the intended quality process.

Inspection characteristics describe what is measured or assessed. Sampling logic determines how much of the lot is inspected. Valuation turns recorded results into accepted or rejected characteristic outcomes. Depending on the process, the specification can come from inspection planning or from production master data such as operations and inspection characteristics.

For example, receiving 100 pieces does not automatically mean inspecting all 100. The inspection setup and sampling procedure can determine a sample, while the inspection plan defines the characteristics to record. The quantity in the logistics document, the quantity represented by the inspection lot, and the quantity physically sampled answer different questions.

Three process shapes

Procurement, production, and delivery reuse QM concepts in different ways.

The inspection lot is common. The trigger and downstream consequence are not.

GRprocurement

Goods receipt inspection

A supplier receipt can create an inspection lot when QM is active for the material and the goods-movement context is configured for inspection.

Quality side

The lot receives its inspection specification, results are recorded, and a usage decision closes the quality assessment.

Logistics side

If the lot is stock-relevant, the inspected quantity can be posted to the appropriate stock category as part of completing the process.

03production

Inspection during production

Releasing a production or process order can create an in-process inspection lot when the relevant inspection type is active.

Quality side

Operations and inspection characteristics from production master data can become the basis for recording quality results during execution.

Important boundary

An in-process inspection and a goods-receipt inspection are different controls. Closing the in-process lot does not automatically mean that finished-goods stock has been posted.

SDdelivery

Delivery inspection

QM can create an inspection lot for a delivery item when the SD/QM control settings and material inspection data require it.

Quality side

The inspection records whether the delivered material meets the required quality criteria and can use customer-specific quality data where configured.

Sales side

The delivery process consumes the quality outcome; it does not replace the inspection lot as the quality object.

Usage decision and stock

A quality decision can change stock, but only when the lot is stock-relevant.

This boundary explains many cases where a user expects a material movement simply because a usage decision was made.

The usage decision records the final quality disposition of an inspection lot. SAP can also use the selected decision code for follow-up processing. For a stock-relevant lot, stock posting can be performed while the usage decision is being processed and, while open quantities remain, can also be completed before or after the decision. The resulting inventory posting is recorded in a material document.

Non-stock-relevant lots do not offer that QM stock-posting function. A manually created sample inspection is a simple example: the inspection can produce valid quality evidence and a usage decision without owning an inventory quantity. This is why “usage decision made” and “stock released” should never be treated as synonyms.

inventory_2

Useful split: QM decides whether the inspected material is acceptable for its intended use. Inventory Management records the stock state created by a goods movement. In warehouse-managed scenarios, EWM can add a separate physical-execution layer around that stock change.

When the process stops

Find the missing transition instead of forcing the stock.

“QM blocked the material” is a symptom. The useful diagnosis is the first quality state that differs from the intended process.

Cross-process ownership

QM controls the inspection; neighboring processes still own their documents.

This division of responsibility is more useful than treating QM as an isolated module.

Reasoning across the chain

Read the process from the business consequence back to the inspection.

This is usually faster than starting from whichever QM screen a user happened to open.

If a supplier receipt is still unavailable for consumption, begin with the stock state and material document. Then find the related inspection lot and ask why it was created, which inspection type applies, whether the specification and results are complete, whether a usage decision exists, and whether a stock posting is still open. Each step has a different owner and a different correction.

The same logic works in production and delivery scenarios. Start with the business effect, identify the quality object that should authorize the next state, and verify the evidence behind that decision. This keeps quality control auditable and prevents a logistics workaround from becoming a hidden process defect.
