Deep vertical / Sales Pricing

Pricing is a determination chain,
not a condition record.

SAP Sales pricing builds a commercial value from document context, a pricing procedure, condition types, access sequences, condition records, and calculation rules. When a price looks wrong, the fastest route is usually to find the first place where that chain differs from the expected design.

Core distinction: the pricing procedure defines what may be calculated; condition records provide business values; the document supplies the runtime context. A valid record in the database proves only that the record exists.

The pricing model

The system first chooses a procedure, then works through its conditions.

Current SAP documentation describes pricing procedure determination from the sales area, the customer pricing procedure, and the document pricing procedure. The resulting pricing procedure defines the condition types that can participate and the sequence in which they are processed.

Document context→ Pricing procedure→ Condition type→ Access sequence→ Condition record→ Price element

The distinction matters during support. If the wrong pricing procedure was selected, adding another condition record cannot repair the real cause. If the correct procedure and condition type are present but no record is found, the problem has moved further down the chain into the access sequence, runtime key, validity, or master data.

SAP's current Example of Pricing Process shows this same sequence explicitly: determine the pricing procedure, read its condition types, follow the assigned access sequence, and use the valid condition record that matches the runtime values.

Condition technique

An access sequence is a search strategy.

A condition type can use an access sequence to search several condition tables in a defined order. Each table represents a key combination such as customer and material, a broader customer group, or a more general material price.

Suppose a list price first searches customer + material, then price list + currency + material, then material. If the first access finds nothing and the second finds a valid record, pricing can use the second result. An exclusive access can stop the search after the first successful match. The result therefore depends on the actual runtime key, not on which record looks most specific when viewed separately.

Condition records are pricing master data. They hold values such as prices, discounts, surcharges, freight, or tax-related amounts together with their key fields and validity. SAP's current Sales documentation also supports APIs for condition records, pricing procedures, condition types, access sequences, condition tables, and the pricing field catalog. That separation is useful: the master data and the determination design are related, but they are not the same object.

Calculation

Finding a record is only the start of the calculation.

The pricing procedure controls how individual price elements interact. Requirements can decide whether a condition is relevant; scales can change a rate; formulas can change the base or amount; subtotals can feed later conditions; and condition exclusion can suppress one result in favor of another.

This is why the useful evidence is the complete price element, not only its rate. A 10% discount can be technically correct and still produce the wrong value if its base is wrong. A condition can be found but inactive. A manual condition can coexist with automatic pricing. Group conditions can also make one item's pricing depend on quantities or values across related items.

Rate

What amount, percentage, or quantity-based rate was determined?

Base

Which value or quantity did the condition use for its calculation?

Interaction

Did a requirement, scale, formula, exclusion rule, subtotal, or group condition change the final effect?

One condition, one reasoning chain

Read a pricing result from permission to amount.

A rate alone never explains a condition result. We need five questions in order. This replaces the separate mandatory Anatomy reading path.

  1. Place: Is the expected condition type in the active pricing procedure, and does its requirement allow it to participate?
  2. Behavior: What reusable behavior does the condition type define: calculation type, manual behavior, scale basis, plus/minus effect, grouping, or another rule?
  3. Source: Where does the value come from: a valid condition record, manual entry, formula, or another supported source?
  4. Base: Which quantity or value is the rate applied to? A correct percentage on the wrong base is still a wrong price.
  5. Interaction: Do scales, group conditions, exclusion, supplements, statistical behavior, or another condition change whether the result is active and how it contributes?

Compact example: a 5% discount can be found correctly and still produce the wrong amount if the base is wrong, the scale level is different, or an exclusion rule deactivates it. Compare working and failing documents at these five layers before changing condition records.

Configuration path

Configure the rule in layers, then test its lifecycle.

Pricing configuration is easier to review when every layer has one job. This replaces the separate mandatory Configuration Playbook reading path.

  1. Business rule: state who receives the price or discount, for which product and context, from which date, and under which quantity or value condition.
  2. Search key: choose the business fields that identify the commercial record. Do not add dimensions that the business cannot maintain reliably.
  3. Condition type: define the reusable behavior of the price, surcharge, discount, or statistical value.
  4. Access logic: define how SAP searches from more specific to more general records where condition technique is used.
  5. Procedure row: place the condition in the pricing procedure and define sequence, eligibility, subtotals, requirements, and calculation controls.
  6. Commercial data: maintain rates, validity periods, currencies, units, and scales separately from the configuration that interprets them.
  7. Lifecycle test: test creation, quantity/date changes, copying to follow-on documents, billing repricing where relevant, cancellation/correction, and one negative case.

Lead rule: do not solve a master-data problem with configuration, and do not solve a configuration problem with custom code. Use an extension only when the standard model cannot express the rule cleanly and the deployment model supports the extension point.

A concrete example

“The condition record exists, but the sales order has no price.”

This symptom is useful because it shows why pricing should be read from the document outward rather than from master data inward.

First confirm which pricing procedure the document actually uses. Then check whether the expected condition type is in that procedure and whether its requirement allows it to run. If the condition type uses an access sequence, pricing analysis should show which accesses were attempted and the field values used for each key. Only then does the existence, validity, currency, unit, and scale of the condition record become decisive.

If the record is found, continue into the calculation. A zero or unexpected value may come from the condition base, a scale, a formula, an exclusion rule, or another condition that changes the result. The diagnosis is complete when we can name the first pricing decision that differs from the expected design—not when we have merely found a table entry that looks plausible.

Order and billing

The invoice does not have to repeat the order's pricing calculation.

When a billing document is created with reference to an earlier document, copying control decides how relevant data moves into Billing. Pricing is one of those controls.

SAP's 2025 FPS01 documentation states that, for delivery-related billing, quantities can come from the delivery while prices can come from the sales order. The same copying-control layer can also specify whether pricing is copied or redetermined according to the configured pricing type. Correct pricing in the order therefore does not prove that the billing document will have the same price elements.

This boundary becomes especially important when a billing document has a different pricing date, tax context, custom field, manual condition, or repricing rule. Compare the order and billing document as two pricing contexts and identify the first difference. The Billing guide covers the broader copying-control and invoice lifecycle.

Extensibility

Extend the pricing model at a defined point, not wherever code is convenient.

Pricing extensions are deployment- and scenario-specific. The important design question is which input or calculation the standard model cannot express, and which supported extension point owns that gap.

In SAP S/4HANA Cloud, released key-user extensibility supports custom fields for Sales and Billing pricing communication and custom logic for selected pricing functions such as requirements, condition base values, and condition amounts. Classic and private-edition landscapes can also contain established pricing routines and enhancement patterns. Those mechanisms should not be described as interchangeable across editions.

A custom pricing field also needs an end-to-end path. The business value must exist in the document context, be available to pricing communication, be exposed through the pricing field catalog where required, participate in the condition-table or calculation design, and be populated in every runtime context that depends on it. Creating the field alone does not make it a pricing input.

Process boundaries

Pricing owns the commercial value, not every downstream consequence.

Several neighboring processes consume pricing results, but they answer different questions.

Sales document

Which pricing procedure and runtime context apply, and which price elements are calculated?

Billing

Which pricing elements are copied, retained, or redetermined when the invoice is created?

Finance

How do the resulting billing values feed account determination, receivables, revenue, tax, and other accounting effects?

If the commercial price is correct but the accounting result is wrong, the investigation has crossed out of Pricing. Continue with FI/CO for Logistics rather than changing condition records.
